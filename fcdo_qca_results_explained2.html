What Makes FCDO Programmes Succeed?

A Configurational Analysis of 312 Programmes

Lucas Sempé

2025-12-01

Part 1: The Question

The Traditional Question

“Which factors predict success?”

Regression logic: each factor in isolation

Problem: Assumes factors work independently — but in reality, success depends on combinations

  • Traditional programme evaluation uses regression analysis

  • Each feature is examined separately, as if it works independently.

The Configurational Question

“What combinations of factors lead to success?”

QCA logic: configurations, not individual effects

Key insight: Multiple pathways can lead to the same outcome (equifinality)

What we’re doing differently:

  • Our approach (Qualitative Comparative Analysis or QCA) asks a different question: “What combinations of features do successful programmes have in common?” This matters because in real life, programme features interact - funding flexibility might only help when combined with strong monitoring systems.

  • Think of it like cooking: regression asks “does adding salt improve dishes on average?” QCA asks “what combinations of ingredients make a great dish?” Salt alone isn’t enough - you need the right recipe.

  • “Equifinality” is a key concept: there can be multiple different recipes that lead to the same successful outcome. Not all good programmes look the same.

What We Want to Know

Which programme features lead to sucess?

But “success” means different things:

Outcome What it measures How common in FCDO reports?
Exceptional Performance A++/A+ overall rating Rare (16%)
Impact Achievement Achieved intended impact Common (80%)
Sustainability Benefits will continue Common (69%)
Equity Outcomes Reached marginalized groups Moderate (34%)

Why multiple outcomes matter:

  • FCDO programmes are rated on a scale from A++ (exceptional) to C (poor). We’re interested in what makes programmes land at the top.

  • “Exceptional Performance” (A++/A+) is rare - only 16% of programmes.

  • “Impact Achievement” is common (80%) - most programmes achieve their stated goals.

  • “Sustainability” asks: will the benefits continue after FCDO funding ends? This is crucial for value-for-money.

  • “Equity Outcomes” is about reaching marginalized groups - women, disabled people, ethnic minorities, the very poor. Only 34% of programmes clearly demonstrate this.

  • Each outcome may require different ingredients, which is why we analyze them separately.

The Theoretical Debate

📋 Results-Based Management
• Clear logframes and theories of change
• Fixed targets set at baseline
• Implementation fidelity to approved plans
• Variance requires explanation
• Accountability through predetermined metrics
"Plan well, then execute the plan"
🔄 Adaptive Management
• Iterative adjustment to emerging evidence
• Learning from implementation
• Flexibility to respond to context
• Regular strategy revision
• Accountability through adaptation quality
"Start, learn, adapt, improve"

The Question: Which approach leads to better development outcomes? Or do they work together?

I framed this analysis looking at two public management schools:

  • Results-based management

  • Adaptive Management

How we processed 312 programmes:

  • Traditional approach: A researcher reads each Business Case (the proposal document) and Annual Review (the progress report), then manually codes whether each of our 14 features is present. At roughly 1 hours per document, that’s 2500 hours of researcher time - about 2 years of full-time work.

  • Our approach: We used LLMs (AI systems like GPT-4) with carefully designed prompts to extract information systematically. This took about 20 seconds per document.

  • Key advantages: (1) Consistency - the AI applies the same criteria every time, unlike humans who might drift or get tired. (2) Speed - 60x faster. (3) Traceability - the AI provides quotes from the documents to support each score.

  • Important: This isn’t fully automated. Humans designed the extraction criteria, validated the outputs, and made final decisions on edge cases. The AI is a research assistant, not a replacement for researcher judgment.

How the extraction actually works:

  • For each programme feature (like “Strategic Clarity”), we gave the AI a clear definition and examples of what to look for.

  • The AI reads the document and provides three things: (1) A score from 0 to 1, (2) A direct quote from the document supporting that score, (3) A justification explaining the reasoning.

  • In this example, the IFFIm programme (International Finance Facility for Immunisation) got a score of 0.67 for Strategic Clarity. The AI found clear outcome indicators (“vaccinate 300 million children”) but noted there was no detailed Theory of Change diagram.

  • The quote-and-justification approach means we can audit every single score. If something looks wrong, we can go back to the source document and check. This transparency is crucial for credibility.

  • Scores are “fuzzy” (0, 0.33, 0.67, or 1.00) rather than just yes/no, which captures nuance. But for the main analysis, we convert to binary: 0.67 or above = feature present, below = feature absent.

Part 3: The Method

What is QCA?

Qualitative Comparative Analysis asks:

“What combinations of conditions are associated with an outcome?”


Traditional regression

  • Effect of X on Y, controlling for Z
  • Assumes variables work independently
  • “On average, funding flexibility increases impact by 0.3 points”

QCA approach

  • What combinations lead to success?
  • Conditions work together
  • “Funding flexibility + local embedding + GESI focus → equity outcomes”

QCA in plain English:

  • Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA) was developed by sociologist Charles Ragin in the 1980s. It bridges the gap between qualitative case studies (deep but not generalizable) and quantitative analysis (generalizable but shallow).

  • Regression says: “On average, programmes with flexible funding score 0.3 points higher” - but this hides the fact that flexibility might only help in certain combinations.

  • QCA says: “Here are the specific recipes that work: Recipe 1 is flexibility + local embedding + GESI. Recipe 2 is rigorous selection + capacity building.” These are actual combinations found in successful programmes.

  • QCA is particularly good for messy, complex social phenomena where:

    • Multiple factors matter simultaneously
    • The same outcome can be reached different ways (equifinality)
    • Context matters - what works in one setting may not work elsewhere
    • You have medium-sized datasets (15-300 cases)

  • Development programmes fit this perfectly - they’re complex interventions in varied contexts where simple “X causes Y” claims rarely hold.

The Key Question

Which conditions matter for success?

NECESSARY
"Can't succeed without it"
SUFFICIENT
"If present, always succeeds"
QCA FINDS
Both types

QCA finds both: necessary (must-haves) AND sufficient (guarantees success)

Necessary vs Sufficient - the key distinction:

Necessary conditions = “You can’t succeed without it, but having it doesn’t guarantee success” - Example: A passport is necessary to travel internationally. Without one, you definitely can’t go. But having a passport doesn’t mean you will travel - you also need money, time, a destination, etc. - In our data: If 100% of successful programmes have a certain feature, that feature is necessary. It’s a prerequisite.

Sufficient conditions = “If you have it, success is guaranteed (or very likely)” - Example: A winning lottery ticket is sufficient for getting money. If you have one, you will get paid. But there are other ways to get money - you don’t need a winning ticket. - In our data: If a certain combination of features leads to success 90%+ of the time, that combination is sufficient.

Why the Venn diagrams help: - Necessary: Success (small circle) is inside the Condition (big circle) - you can’t have success without the condition - Sufficient: Condition (small circle) is inside Success (big circle) - whenever you have the condition, you’re in the success zone

In development: - We want to find necessary conditions (minimum requirements for any programme) - AND sufficient “recipes” (combinations that reliably produce success)

The Key Insight: Prevalence Shapes Our Analysis

When outcomes are RARE

Necessity analysis is informative

  • If 100% of successes have a feature, that’s meaningful
  • Finding “recipes” is hard (few successful cases)

Example: Exceptional Performance

When outcomes are COMMON

Sufficiency analysis is informative

  • Many conditions will seem “necessary” by chance
  • Finding reliable “recipes” is easier

Example: Impact Achievement

Why this matters

We use different analytical strategies for different outcomes based on how common they are.

See: Schneider & Wagemann (2012) on “trivialness”; Braumoeller & Goertz (2000) on necessary conditions methodology

Why outcome prevalence changes our analysis strategy:

When outcomes are RARE (like Exceptional Performance at 16%): - Necessity analysis is very informative. If we look at the small group of exceptional programmes and find they ALL have a certain feature, that’s a strong signal. - Sufficiency analysis is hard because we have few “positive” cases to analyze. With only 50 exceptional programmes out of 312, finding reliable patterns is statistically challenging.

When outcomes are COMMON (like Impact Achievement at 80%): - Necessity analysis becomes less informative. If 80% of programmes achieve impact, then any common feature will appear “necessary” almost by chance. For example, if 90% of programmes have M&E systems, and 80% achieve impact, the overlap will be high even if M&E doesn’t matter. - Sufficiency analysis works better because we have many successful cases to find patterns in.

Practical implication: - For Exceptional Performance, we focus on “what do the elite have that others don’t?” (necessity) - For Impact Achievement, we focus on “what combinations reliably produce success?” (sufficiency)

This is a methodological subtlety that affects how we interpret our results.

Understanding QCA Metrics

🎯 Necessity Consistency
Question: When outcome is present, is the condition also present?
Threshold: ≥0.90 (condition in 90%+ of successful cases)
High = condition is a "must-have"
📏 Sufficiency Consistency (incl.cut)
Question: When condition pattern is present, does outcome follow?
Our threshold: incl.cut = 0.85 (85% of cases succeed)
High = pathway reliably produces success
📊 Coverage
Question: What % of successful cases does this pathway explain?
Our solutions: 65-75% coverage (3 valid outcomes)
High = pathways capture most routes to success
🔢 Frequency Threshold
Question: Minimum cases to consider a pathway empirically grounded?
Our thresholds: 2 (rare outcomes) or 3 (common outcomes)
Balances pattern detection with empirical support

Key thresholds: Necessity ≥0.90 | Sufficiency (incl.cut) ≥0.85 | n.cut = 2-3 depending on outcome prevalence

Understanding the key numbers:

Necessity Consistency (threshold ≥0.90): - This asks: “Of all successful programmes, what percentage have this feature?” - If 90%+ of successful programmes have a feature, we call it necessary. - Why 90% not 100%? Real-world data is messy. A few exceptions don’t disprove a general rule.

Sufficiency Consistency (threshold ≥0.85): - This asks: “Of all programmes with this combination of features, what percentage succeed?” - If 85%+ succeed, we call that combination a reliable “recipe” for success. - We use 0.85, a standard threshold in QCA literature.

Coverage: - This asks: “What percentage of all successful programmes does this recipe explain?” - A recipe might be 100% reliable but only apply to 5% of cases - useful but limited. - We want recipes that are both reliable (high consistency) AND broadly applicable (reasonable coverage).

Frequency threshold (n.cut): - How many programmes must follow a pattern before we trust it? - We use 2 for rare outcomes (can’t be too picky with limited data) and 3 for common outcomes. - This prevents us from drawing conclusions from one-off cases.

How QCA works (I)

Enhanced Standard Analysis (ESA) is a structured 6-step QCA workflow that systematically tests necessity, builds truth tables, identifies contradictions, and derives the Enhanced solution—the recommended “recipes” that reliably explain success while excluding problematic configurations.


The Enhanced Solution identifies the minimal combination of conditions that reliably produce the outcome, while explicitly excluding contradictory configurations (where the same condition profile leads to mixed outcomes).

The Enhanced Standard Analysis (ESA) - our analytical workflow:

  • ESA is a systematic 6-step process developed by QCA methodologists to ensure rigorous, transparent analysis.

  • The key output is the “Enhanced Solution” - this is the set of reliable recipes for success.

  • Why “enhanced”? Because it explicitly handles contradictions. If two programmes have identical features but different outcomes (one succeeds, one fails), that tells us something is missing from our model. The enhanced solution excludes these problematic cases rather than pretending they don’t exist.

  • Think of it like medical diagnosis: if two patients have identical symptoms but different diseases, a good diagnostic protocol flags this ambiguity rather than guessing.

  • The “minimal” aspect means we simplify recipes to their essential ingredients. If programmes succeed with features A+B+C, and also with features A+B (without C), then C isn’t actually needed - the minimal recipe is just A+B.

How QCA works (II)

Step What it does What to look for
Step 1: Necessity Tests if conditions are prerequisites Consistency ≥90% means “almost always present when outcome occurs”
Step 2: Truth Table Construction Maps all observed configurations How cases distribute across condition combinations
Step 3: Contradiction Analysis Identifies configs with mixed outcomes How many configs show both success and failure
Step 4: Enhanced Solution Derives reliable pathways (excludes contradictions) The “recipes” for success
Step 5: SUIN Check Tests for INUS conditions Does any condition appear in ALL pathways?
Step 6: Summary Consolidates findings Key necessary conditions + sufficient pathways

Walking through the 6 steps:

Step 1 - Necessity: First, we check each feature individually. Is there any feature that’s present in 90%+ of successful programmes? If yes, it’s a necessary condition - a prerequisite for success.

Step 2 - Truth Table: We create a table showing every observed combination of features. With 14 features, there are theoretically 16,384 possible combinations (2^14). In practice, we only observe 24-56 combinations in our data - most theoretical combinations don’t exist in real programmes.

Step 3 - Contradictions: We identify combinations that appear in both successful and unsuccessful programmes. If 10 programmes have features A+B+C and 7 succeed while 3 fail, that’s a contradiction - something else we’re not measuring must explain the difference.

Step 4 - Enhanced Solution: We use Boolean logic to find the minimal combinations that reliably lead to success, explicitly excluding contradictory configurations.

Step 5 - SUIN Check: We test for “INUS” conditions - features that appear in EVERY pathway to success. If every recipe contains ingredient X, then X is special (even if it’s not sufficient by itself).

Step 6 - Summary: We consolidate findings into actionable insights.

Understanding Truth Table Classifications

When building truth tables, each observed configuration of conditions is classified:

✓ Sufficient (OUT=1)
Configurations that reliably produce the outcome. When programmes have this combination of conditions, they consistently succeed.
Consistency ≥0.85
✗ Insufficient (OUT=0)
Configurations that do not reliably produce the outcome. Programmes with these condition combinations rarely or never succeed.
Consistency <0.85 with no positive cases, OR too few cases to evaluate
⚠ Contradictory
Configurations with mixed outcomes—some programmes succeed, others fail, despite having identical conditions. Signals unmeasured factors.
0 < Consistency < 0.85 with cases on both sides
? Logical Remainders
Configurations that are logically possible but never observed. With 14 conditions, 16,384 combinations exist but only ~24-56 appear in data.
n = 0 (no cases)

Why contradictions matter: The Enhanced solution excludes contradictory configurations to avoid unreliable causal inferences. High contradiction rates suggest important unmeasured factors.

Reading truth table results:

  • Sufficient (OUT=1): These are the “winning” combinations. Programmes with these feature profiles consistently succeed. This is what we’re looking for.

  • Insufficient (OUT=0): These combinations don’t work. Programmes with these features don’t succeed - useful to know what to avoid.

  • Contradictory: Same features, different outcomes. This is actually valuable information - it tells us our 14 features don’t capture everything. Maybe leadership quality, political timing, or luck matters but isn’t in our data.

  • Logical Remainders: Combinations that are logically possible but never observed. With 14 binary features, there are 16,384 possible combinations. Real programmes only exhibit 24-56 of these. Most combinations simply don’t occur in practice.

The contradiction rate is a quality indicator: - Few contradictions = our features capture most of what matters - Many contradictions = important factors are missing from our model - Exceptional Performance has many contradictions (21 out of 56) - we’re missing something important - Equity has few contradictions (7 out of 56) - our features capture what matters

  2. “~LocalEmbedded * EquityGESI” - NOT locally embedded + GESI. Interesting finding: sometimes external pressure is needed for equity. Local systems may perpetuate existing inequalities.

  3. “OutputsAccessible * EquityGESI * InstabilityResponse” - Open access + GESI + crisis adaptation. Responding to crises through an equity lens with open outputs.

Policy implication: If FCDO cares about equity outcomes, GESI must be mandatory and meaningful - not a checkbox. It’s the one feature where box-ticking is demonstrably insufficient and genuine integration is required.

📝

90% claim crisis response. Are they actually adapting, or just saying they adapted?
🔄
System Circularity
FCDO rewards ToC & MEL. Programmes with these score highly. Finding or tautology?

Unmeasured Factors
Leadership, luck, political windows — we can't capture these in the data.

Self-Reported Data
Business Cases & ARs are self-reported. Incentives to present favourably.

Important caveats to acknowledge:

Rhetorical Adaptation: - 90% of programmes claim to have adapted to crises - But are they actually adapting, or just writing that they did in their reports? - Self-reported data has obvious incentive problems - We’re measuring what programmes SAY, not what they DO

System Circularity: - FCDO’s scoring system rewards Theories of Change and M&E systems - Programmes that have these get rated higher - We then find that high-rated programmes have these features - Is this a finding or a tautology? - Partly genuine (ToC matters), partly an artifact of how scoring works

Unmeasured Factors: - Leadership quality, team chemistry, luck, political windows - These matter enormously but aren’t in programme documents - Our 14 features don’t capture everything (obviously) - The high contradiction rate for Exceptional Performance shows this

Self-Reported Data: - Business Cases are proposals - they present plans optimistically - Annual Reviews are progress reports - programmes want to look good - Both have incentives to present favourably - Independent evaluations would be more reliable but aren’t available at scale

These caveats don’t invalidate the findings, but they inform how we interpret them.

The Metrics Explained

Consistency: “How reliable is this pattern?”

\[\text{Consistency} = \frac{\text{Cases with condition AND outcome}}{\text{Cases with condition}}\]

  • 0.85+ = Reliable (85%+ of cases with this pattern succeed)
  • Below 0.85 = Too many exceptions to trust

Coverage: “How much does this explain?”

\[\text{Coverage} = \frac{\text{Cases with condition AND outcome}}{\text{All successful cases}}\]

  • High coverage = This pattern explains many successes
  • Low coverage = This is just one of many routes to success

Technical appendix for those who want the details:

Consistency formula: - Numerator: Programmes that have both the condition AND the successful outcome - Denominator: All programmes that have the condition - If this ratio is high (85%+), the condition reliably leads to success

Example: If 100 programmes have “OutputsAccessible” and 88 of them achieved impact, consistency = 88/100 = 0.88. This means 88% of the time, having accessible outputs led to impact. That’s reliable enough to recommend.

Coverage formula: - Numerator: Same as above (programmes with condition AND outcome) - Denominator: All successful programmes - If this ratio is high, this pathway explains a large share of successes

Example: If 250 programmes achieved impact and 88 of them had accessible outputs, coverage = 88/250 = 35.2%. This means accessible outputs explains about a third of impact successes. Other pathways explain the rest.

Why both matter: - High consistency, low coverage = reliable but niche (works but only for some) - Low consistency, high coverage = common but unreliable (many programmes have it but it doesn’t guarantee success) - We want high consistency (reliable) AND reasonable coverage (explains enough cases to be useful)

The trade-off: Very specific recipes have higher consistency but lower coverage. Very broad recipes have higher coverage but lower consistency. We aim for the “sweet spot” of both being good enough.
